# Practice Translation Skill

## Initial questions before starting

Before rewriting or translating the practice, ask the user for any missing context:

1. Who is this for?
   Example: funders, actors, agents, producers, audiences, institutions, workshop participants.

2. What is the purpose of the text?
   Example: website copy, funding application, artist statement, email, workshop description.

3. What should the audience understand or feel after reading it?

4. What must not be lost from the original voice or practice?

5. Is there a concrete example, moment, action, or transformation that shows what actually happens?

## When to invoke

Use this skill when:

- a practitioner struggles to clearly explain their work
- language feels overly abstract, academic, or vague
- communication needs adapting for a specific audience
- nuance or emotional truth risks being lost in translation
- a user wants clarity without flattening the work into marketing language

Typical inputs may include:

- artist statements
- workshop descriptions
- funding applications
- rehearsal notes
- interview transcripts
- website copy
- process reflections
- voice notes or rough thoughts

## When NOT to invoke

Do not use this skill when:

- the task is primarily SEO optimisation
- the user wants generic marketing copy
- the user requests highly corporate sales language
- the task is technical documentation unrelated to practice translation
- the goal is persuasion without fidelity to the original practice

## Inputs expected

The skill works best when given:

- raw practitioner language
- intended audience
- context for the work
- examples, metaphors, or lived details
- existing drafts that feel inaccurate or overly polished

Optional but useful:

- references the practitioner feels represent the work well
- examples of language they dislike
- emotional tone they want preserved

## Step-by-step process

1. Identify what the practitioner actually does.
2. Identify who the communication is for.
3. Identify what the audience needs to understand, feel, or do.
4. Find where the current language is vague, inflated, academic, or too abstract.
5. Extract concrete details: actions, gestures, sensations, examples, participant shifts, settings, or moments.
6. Identify what shifts, changes, or becomes possible through the process or experience.
7. Translate the meaning into clearer language while staying close to the practitioner’s intent.
8. Generate 2–3 framing options when useful:
   - public-facing / website version
   - funder or institution-facing version
   - practitioner-facing or emotionally resonant version
9. Briefly explain the translation choices.
10. Flag anything that may still need clarification from the practitioner.

## Expected outputs

Outputs should typically include:

- a revised version of the text
- brief explanation of translation choices
- optional alternative framings for different audiences
- notes on where meaning may still need clarification

Outputs should prioritise:

- clarity
- specificity
- fidelity to the practitioner
- emotional and conceptual integrity

## Output format

When producing rewritten text, use this structure where appropriate:

### 1. Plain-language version

A clear version that a non-specialist can understand.

### 2. Audience-specific version

A version adapted for the stated audience, such as funders, agents, producers, collaborators, or participants.

### 3. What changed

A short explanation of what was clarified, made more concrete, or preserved from the original.

### 4. Remaining ambiguities

Anything that still requires clarification from the practitioner.

## Quality check

Before finalising the output, check that:

- It includes at least one concrete action, method, example, or observable detail.
- A non-specialist could understand what actually happens.
- Claims are specific, honest, and not exaggerated.
- The practitioner's voice is still recognisable.
- Generic marketing language and creative-industry buzzwords have been removed.
- The output explains what changes for the participant, audience, or collaborator.
- Clarity has improved without flattening complexity.
- The level of detail matches the intended audience.
- The tone feels human, grounded, and specific.

If any criterion is not met, revise the output before presenting it.

## Success criteria

A translation is considered successful when:

- a non-specialist can explain back what the practice does
- at least one concrete example or action is included
- the practitioner recognises their own voice in the result
- the audience can understand what changes through the process
- no claims have been added that are not supported by the original material

## Anti-patterns

Do not:

- Replace specificity with abstract creative-sector language.
- Use buzzwords that sound meaningful but do not explain what happens.
- Turn ambiguity into fake certainty.
- Overclaim transformation, healing, confidence, safety, or emotional impact.
- Make the writing sound more professional at the cost of truth.
- Flatten embodied, relational, or process-led work into generic marketing copy.
- Invent evidence, outcomes, testimonials, or audience responses.
- Use polished AI language that the practitioner would not recognise as their own.
